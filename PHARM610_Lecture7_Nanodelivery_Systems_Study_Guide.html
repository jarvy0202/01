0. The Big Picture: Why Put a Drug in a Nanocarrier?

A free drug injected into the blood goes wherever its own chemistry takes it. Doxorubicin, for example, spreads into almost every tissue, including the heart, so it causes heart damage. The idea of nano-drug delivery is to package the drug inside a tiny carrier (roughly 10–200 nm). If the drug stays inside long enough, it travels where the carrier goes rather than where the drug would go on its own.

Nanocarriers are too big to leak out of normal blood vessels. Tumors and inflamed tissues have leaky vessels and poor lymphatic drainage, so nanocarriers can leak in and stay there. This is the EPR effect, covered in the previous lecture. The result is more drug at the disease site and less in healthy organs.

This lecture covers the main carrier types:

  • Cyclodextrins: early and simple.
  • Liposomes: the most successful type, and the core of the lecture.
  • Polymeric nanoparticles.
  • Polymeric micelles.

For each type you need to know how it's made, how drug is loaded, and which products reached the clinic, including why some succeeded and others failed.

Terms you need

1. Cyclodextrins

Cyclodextrins (CDs) were among the first nano-delivery systems. A CD is a ring of glucose (glucopyranose) units shaped like a hollow, truncated cone or bucket.

Typeα-CDβ-CDγ-CD
Glucose units678
  • The outside is hydrophilic (OH groups face the water), so the CD dissolves in water. The inner cavity is hydrophobic.
  • A poorly soluble drug slips into the cavity and forms an inclusion complex (1:1 or 1:2 drug : CD). The water-soluble CD carries it into solution, so CDs are solubilizing agents.
  • Groups can be attached to the CD rim to change its affinity for different drugs, e.g., 2-hydroxypropyl-β-CD and sulfobutylether-β-CD.
  • Many CD products reached the clinic as solubilizers. The product list is FYI.
Why did CDs fail for drug targeting? Complexation is a fast, reversible equilibrium. After injection the complex is diluted in blood and the drug is released very quickly. The drug never travels with the carrier, so the carrier can't direct it anywhere.

2. Liposomes: Structure, Making Them, Loading Drug

2.1 What a liposome is

A phospholipid has a hydrophilic head (a phosphate plus a group such as choline) attached through glycerol to two hydrophobic fatty-acid tails. In water these molecules line up tail-to-tail to hide their tails from water, forming a bilayer. The bilayer then closes on itself into a hollow sphere, or vesicle. That vesicle is a liposome. Phospholipids can also form micelles, but liposomes come from the bilayer arrangement.

Key point: Liposomes can carry hydrophilic drugs (in the aqueous core), hydrophobic drugs (in the lipid bilayer) and amphiphilic drugs (at the interface between the two).

2.2 Building blocks

  • Phospholipids, usually a mixture:
    • Phosphatidylcholine (PC, lecithin)
    • Phosphatidylethanolamine (PE, cephalin)
    • Phosphatidylserine (PS)
    • Phosphatidylinositol (PI)
    • Phosphatidylglycerol (PG)
  • Fatty-acid tails set the phase transition temperature (Tm), the temperature at which the bilayer switches from a rigid gel to a fluid state. Longer, saturated tails give a higher Tm and a tighter, less leaky membrane.
  • Head groups add special functions:
    • Cationic lipids (DOTAP, DOTMA): positive charge helps the liposome fuse with cell and endosomal membranes.
    • pH-dependent (ionizable) lipids (e.g., DLin-MC3-DMA): help endosomal escape and are used to load nucleic acids such as RNA (the basis of the mRNA vaccine LNPs).
    • PEG-lipids (DSPE-PEG2000): give stealth properties.
  • Cholesterol is almost always added. It wedges between phospholipids and increases bilayer rigidity, which reduces leakage of the encapsulated drug.

2.3 Preparation methods

① Thin-film hydration is the classic method. It's used less today.

Step 1Dissolve lipids (+ lipophilic drug) in organic solvent: chloroform, dichloromethane, or chloroform/alcohol
Step 2Remove solvent on a rotary evaporator → thin lipid film on the flask wall
Step 3Add aqueous phase (water/PBS, + hydrophilic drug) and agitate → liposomes form spontaneously (mostly large MLVs)
Step 4Sonication or extrusion → smaller, more uniform vesicles

② Ethanol injection: dissolve the lipid in ethanol and inject it into the aqueous phase. This gives smaller, more homogeneous liposomes directly, often with no need for sonication or extrusion. It's common in the lab.

③ Microfluidics is the newest approach. Organic and aqueous streams mix in tiny channels, which gives better, controlled mixing. The process is automated and scalable to large batches.

2.4 Vesicle types

Which type you get depends on the lipid and the preparation method.

2.6 Loading drug into liposomes

Passive loading means the drug is added during preparation. A drug soluble in the organic solvent goes in with the lipids. A water-soluble drug goes in the hydration buffer.

★ Active (remote) loading works the other way round. The liposome is made first, then incubated with the drug. Something placed inside the liposome beforehand pulls the drug from the outside to the inside and traps it there:

  • a buffer that sets an internal pH, so the drug becomes ionized inside and can't cross back out; or
  • a salt that forms a complex with the drug and makes it precipitate inside.
Doxil example: the liposome contains ammonium sulfate. Doxorubicin enters, forms a complex with sulfate and precipitates inside, so leakage is greatly hindered. The lecturer stressed that this is why Doxil changes drug PK more than any other nanoformulation. (The transcript says "aluminium sulfate"; the correct term is ammonium sulfate.)

2.7 Quality control for generic liposomes

Doxil is now off-patent, so generic companies can make it. Regulators need standards that prove a generic matches the brand product and that batches are uniform. These are the Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs):

  1. Lipid analysis: lipid identity and concentration.
  2. Drug encapsulation: how much drug is inside versus free.
  3. Particle characterization: size, polydispersity (PDI), charge.
  4. Stability: tendency to aggregate, and shelf life.
  5. Drug release: the extent and rate of release.

2.8 Tools: AF4 and in vitro release testing

Field-flow fractionation (AF4, asymmetrical flow FFF) separates and purifies nanoparticles. Particles travel along a thin channel carried by a liquid flow, and a cross-flow pushes them toward one wall. Smaller particles diffuse back into the faster stream and come out first. Some versions add an electric field to separate particles by charge, and others separate by size and density. Its main advantage is that there is no stationary phase, so separation depends only on flow. This is gentle and avoids particles sticking to column packing.

In vitro drug release methods include sample-and-separate, dialysis membrane, continuous flow and reverse dialysis. Dialysis is the most common, and it can be automated by placing dialysis devices in USP apparatus 2 (the paddle apparatus used for tablet dissolution). There are two sampling strategies:

  • Put the formulation in a bag inside the medium and sample the medium over time.
  • If the released drug is too dilute to measure, prepare one dialysis sample per time point. At each time, remove one and measure the drug remaining inside.
Why add surfactant or serum albumin to the release medium? Poorly water-soluble drugs barely dissolve in buffer, so released drug would saturate the medium and release would look artificially slow. Surfactant or albumin solubilizes or binds the drug and keeps sink conditions, meaning the medium is never close to saturation.

2.9 Clinical timeline (FYI, just the landmarks)

Most liposomal products appeared from the mid-1990s, mainly anticancer and antifungal drugs. Landmarks:

  • Doxil/Caelyx (1995)
  • AmBisome (1997)
  • Myocet and Visudyne (2000)
  • Onivyde (2015)
  • Onpattro (2018, lipid–siRNA)
  • mRNA vaccines (2020): lipid nanoparticles made from similar lipids

★ 3. The Core Concept: Release Rate Decides Whether the Carrier Changes PK

This is the most important idea of the lecture. A carrier can only change a drug's PK and biodistribution while the drug is still inside it. Once the drug leaks out, it behaves like a free drug again.

Rapid-release formulations

The first liposomes were conventional ones that held hydrophobic drugs in the lipid bilayer. A drug dissolved in the membrane escapes quickly, so these liposomes give no sustained release, and the drug's PK stays like the free drug's. Slide example: Visudyne (verteporfin, held in the hydrophobic membrane interior).

Sustained-release formulations

Here the drug is encapsulated in the aqueous interior, not in the bilayer, and it leaks out slowly. The lecturer stressed that this is the only situation in which the carrier can change the drug's PK and biodistribution. The drug is "forced" to follow the liposome instead of its own PK.

If drug release is…then PK of the drug ≈
Very slow (e.g., Caelyx/Doxil)PK of the carrier
Very fastPK of the free drug
Sustained (typical)In between: drug released early follows free-drug PK; drug still inside follows carrier PK
Fast release → PK = free drugMixedSlow release → PK = carrier
Lecturer's conclusion: "The slower the release, the more similar the PK and biodistribution of your drug to that of the carrier. That is why slow drug release is a prerequisite for achieving drug targeting."

Why sustained release raises the therapeutic index

Because the drug follows the liposome, it is directed to the site of action. Liposomes leak into tumors and inflamed tissue through the EPR effect, while normal tissues are spared. The result:

  • Less drug reaches healthy tissue, so the dose that causes toxicity goes up.
  • More drug reaches the target, so the dose that gives efficacy goes down.
  • TI = toxic dose ÷ effective dose, so the therapeutic index increases.

★ 4. Conventional vs Stealth Liposomes

4.1 Conventional (classical) liposomes

Conventional liposomes have a bare phospholipid surface. Once injected, blood proteins (opsonins) stick to the "naked" surface. Macrophages of the MPS, mainly Kupffer cells in the liver and macrophages in the spleen, recognize these proteins and destroy the liposomes. As a result, tumor uptake is low.

Main problems:

  1. Short circulation time, so the liposomes fail to reach and localize in diseased tissue.
  2. High uptake into liver and spleen, which can impair these organs with repeated dosing.

Conventional products can still be useful:

  • AmBisome (amphotericin B, an antifungal; SUV liposome; approved 1997).
    • Amphotericin B is amphiphilic, so it sits at the lipid–water interface.
    • Main benefit: reduced nephrotoxicity.
    • The lecturer used it as a sustained-release example, but it is still conventional (not stealth).
  • Myocet: conventional liposomal doxorubicin, with the drug in the aqueous core.

Both lack stealth properties, so both are taken up by mononuclear phagocytic cells.

4.2 Stealth liposomes

1Surface coated with a hydrophilic polymer (PEG)
2PEG binds water → a hydration layer forms around the liposome
3Blood proteins (opsonins) can't adsorb, so the liposome looks like "self"
4Not taken up by Kupffer cells → long-circulating, low liver/spleen accumulation
5With sustained release → drug delivered to solid tumors / inflammation (EPR)
Remember: you need both stealth (to stay in circulation) and sustained release (to keep the drug inside) to get drug targeting.

★ 5. Doxil / Caelyx: The Model Success Story

Doxil (called Caelyx in Canada and Europe) is PEGylated (stealth) liposomal doxorubicin, used for example in ovarian cancer.

5.1 PK changes

Putting doxorubicin in stealth sustained-release liposomes:

  • ↓ Clearance: it isn't eliminated or taken up quickly.
  • ↓ Volume of distribution: the drug stays in the blood.
  • ↑ Half-life
  • ↑ AUC
  • ↑ Distribution to tumors, the overall result of the changes above.

The lecturer said we can expect the same set of changes for any drug in a stealth nanocarrier with sustained release.

Why does Vd drop from 254 L to 4.1 L? Vd = amount of drug in the body ÷ plasma concentration. Free doxorubicin leaves the blood and enters tissues, including normal ones, so plasma concentration is low and Vd is huge (254 L, far bigger than a body). Liposomal doxorubicin is too large to cross the continuous capillaries of normal tissues and, being stealth, isn't taken up by tissue macrophages. The drug is therefore confined to plasma, and Vd = 4.1 L, close to the ~5 L of blood in an average adult.
Why is Doxil still the best nanoformulation? No other product changes drug PK this much. The reason is the internal complexation and precipitation of doxorubicin, which keeps leakage extremely low.

5.2 Toxicity profile

Liposomal drug doesn't pass through normal blood vessels, so many normal tissues are spared:

Side effects that decreaseSide effects that increase
  • Cardiac toxicity
  • Hematological toxicity
  • Hair loss (little or none)
  • Peripheral neuropathy
  • Extravasation (vesicant) injuries
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Mucositis
  • Hand-foot syndrome (palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia, PPE)

The slides compare this shift to giving a drug by continuous infusion instead of a bolus injection: long exposure at lower peak levels.

Why hand-foot syndrome? Hands and feet are pressure points. The pressure is thought to squeeze liposomes out of the small vessels, so drug builds up in the skin there and causes redness, peeling and pain. It appears at doses above about 10–12 mg/m² per week. Management: clinicians delay the next chemotherapy cycle at the first sign of toxicity.

5.3 Onivyde (liposomal irinotecan, about 2015)

  • Irinotecan (CPT-11) is a prodrug of SN-38. SN-38 is about 1000× more potent, but it is so poorly water-soluble that it can't be injected. That is the only reason the prodrug is used.
  • SN-38G (SN-38 glucuronide) is the inactive metabolite.
  • The liposome changes the PK of irinotecan and its metabolites in the same direction as Doxil (↑ AUC, longer t½). On the slide, 120 mg/m² of liposomal irinotecan gives much higher and longer-lasting levels than 300 mg/m² of the free drug.

6. Active Targeting and Triggered Release

The second generation of liposomes carries a targeting ligand on the surface that binds a receptor on the target cells.

6.1 Internalizing vs non-internalizing ligands

  • Internalizing ligand: binds a receptor that is taken into the cell (endocytosis), pulling the liposome inside.
  • Non-internalizing ligand: the liposome just sits next to the cell and releases drug nearby.
  • Studies show internalizing ligands work better in most cases.

Ligand options:

  • Full antibodies, antibody fragments (Fab′, scFv) and nanobodies
  • Aptamers
  • Peptides (e.g., RGD, NGR)
  • Small molecules such as folic acid; also transferrin and lectins

The specific antibody targets (HER2, CD19, etc.) are FYI.

★ 6.2 Does a ligand increase tumor accumulation? No.

  • Evidence shows that adding a ligand does not increase solid-tumor localization. Getting into the tumor is controlled by circulation time and EPR (passive targeting), not by the ligand.
  • A high ligand density can cover and overshadow the PEG stealth layer. Plasma clearance by the liver, spleen and phagocytic cells then goes up, so tumor accumulation can actually go down.
  • What a ligand can change is the intratumoral distribution: where the liposome ends up inside the tumor, e.g., more uptake into cells.

★ 6.3 Binding site barrier

  • The larger the tumor, the greater the barrier to diffusion of the liposomal drug into its core.
  • Micrometastases or targets inside the blood vessels present fewer barriers, so they are better suited to ligand-targeted liposomes.

★ 6.4 Post-insertion technique (how ligands are attached)

1Attach the ligand (e.g., anti-CD19 antibody) to a PEG-phospholipid (e.g., maleimide-PEG-DSPE)
2These ligand-PEG-lipids self-assemble into micelles
3Co-incubate the micelles with preformed conventional or PEGylated liposomes (slide: 37 °C, 1 h)
4Ligand-lipids insert into the outer leaflet → targeted stealth liposome (e.g., anti-CD19 Caelyx)
Why is post-insertion better than mixing the ligand-lipid in from the start? If the ligand-lipid is mixed with the other lipids before film hydration or ethanol injection, many ligands end up on the inner leaflet, facing the liposome interior where they are useless. Post-insertion places them only on the outside, so more ligand is exposed on the surface.

6.5 Triggered release: ThermoDox

  • A temperature-sensitive lipid (lysolipid) is built into the liposome.
  • The idea: the liposome stays stable at 37 °C. When the tumor is heated to 42 °C, the membrane undergoes a phase transition and releases doxorubicin rapidly at the heated site.
  • It created a lot of excitement in the early 2000s.
  • Why it failed: it did not prevent leakage at 37 °C. The drug leaked before reaching the tumor, so it didn't go far in development.

7.2 Two designs: nanocapsule vs nanosphere

★ 7.3 Preparation methods

① In situ polymerization: the particle is built directly from monomers that polymerize in the mixture.

  • Emulsion polymerization: one phase is a water-immiscible organic phase. The initiator sits in one phase and the monomer in the other (or the reverse). The continuous phase can be organic or aqueous. Surfactants prevent aggregation.
  • Interfacial polymerization: polymerization happens at the oil–water interface.

② Coacervation is mostly used for microparticles and produces reservoir systems.

  1. The polymer is dissolved in an organic solvent with drug particles dispersed in it (the drug forms the core).
  2. A non-solvent (usually water) is added. The polymer separates out as a coacervate that deposits around the drug particles.
  3. The solvent is evaporated and the coacervate hardens into a coating.

③ Emulsification / solvent evaporation is the most common method for PLGA particles.

Aqueous phaseWater + PVA (polyvinyl alcohol) or surfactant as stabilizer
Organic phasePolymer in a water-immiscible solvent (e.g., chloroform)
EmulsifyOil and water don't mix → emulsion droplets
EvaporateRemove chloroform → droplets harden into nanoparticles coated with PVA/surfactant
  • O/W (oil-in-water) → for water-insoluble drugs, which dissolve in the oil phase with the polymer.
  • W/O/W (double emulsion) → for water-soluble drugs. The drug sits in an inner water droplet inside the oil droplet, so it isn't lost to the outer water.

④ Solvent displacement (nanoprecipitation) uses a water-miscible organic solvent: acetone or other polar solvents such as THF or acetonitrile. The polymer is dissolved in it and mixed with water. Because the solvent mixes with water, it diffuses away instantly. Water acts as a non-solvent, so the polymer precipitates as particles, and the organic solvent is then evaporated. This is also used for PLGA and gives smaller particles than the emulsion method.

⑤ Precipitation by salting out is like solvent displacement, but particle formation is triggered by electrolytes in the aqueous phase. The salt "salts out" the polymer. The mixture is then diluted with water.

7.5 Abraxane (nab-paclitaxel)

  • Human serum albumin-bound paclitaxel nanoparticles, approved 2005 (breast cancer).
  • Unlike Doxil, it does not change paclitaxel's PK the way a stealth carrier would. Even so, it is clinically effective in several cancers and was a breakthrough when it came out.

7.6 Steric stabilization

Nanoparticles tend to stick together (aggregate). Steric stabilization means putting hydrophilic macromolecules on the particle surface. When two coated particles approach, their hydrated polymer layers physically and thermodynamically resist overlapping (ΔG = ΔH − TΔS; details not required), so the particles stay apart.

  • Physical adsorption (PEO coating): PVA, or PEG-containing block copolymers that adsorb onto the surface:
    • Pluronics: PEO–PPO–PEO triblock
    • PEO–PLA diblock
  • Chemical attachment (PEO grafting): PEG covalently bonded to the surface.

★ 8. Polymeric Micelles

8.1 What they are

The same block copolymers used for steric stabilization can also self-assemble on their own. An amphiphilic block copolymer has one hydrophilic block (usually PEG) and one hydrophobic block. Above the CMC, the chains come together into a core–shell structure, the polymeric micelle.

  • Size about 100 nm. The shell is usually a dense PEG layer, and the core holds the hydrophobic blocks.
  • Uses: dissolving water-insoluble drugs in the core, and potentially targeting poorly soluble drugs.
  • Common polymers:
    • PEO-b-PPO (Pluronic)
    • PEO-b-PLA
    • PEO-b-poly(L-amino acid), e.g., poly(aspartic acid) derivatives

★ 8.2 Why micelles form: thermodynamics and the hydrophobic effect

Self-assembly is driven thermodynamically by the hydrophobic effect. Work through it in steps:

  1. ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. A process is spontaneous (thermodynamically favorable) only if ΔG < 0.
  2. In water, micellization is usually endothermic: ΔH > 0. Enthalpy works against it, so the only way to make ΔG negative is a large increase in entropy (ΔS > 0). Micellization in water is therefore entropy-driven.
  3. Where does the entropy come from? From water, not the polymer. Water molecules form a 3D hydrogen-bond network.
  4. The hydrophilic block can hydrogen-bond with water, but the hydrophobic block can't. It disrupts the water network, and water around it is forced into an ordered, low-entropy arrangement.
  5. To regain entropy, water "expels" the hydrophobic blocks. When the hydrophobic blocks cluster into a core hidden from water, the ordered water is released and the hydrogen-bond network re-forms freely, so water entropy rises.
  6. This entropy gain (TΔS) outweighs the positive ΔH, giving ΔG < 0: micellization is spontaneous.
Contrast: in organic solvents, micellization is enthalpy-driven (exothermic).

★ 8.3 Polymeric micelles vs low-molecular-weight surfactant micelles

Ordinary surfactants (Cremophor EL, SDS, polysorbates) also form micelles, but polymeric micelles hold up much better in the body. There are two reasons:

Polymeric micellesSurfactant micelles
1. CMC (thermodynamic stability)Much lower. The hydrophobic block is a whole macromolecule, so its drive to escape water and associate is much stronger.Higher
2. Kinetic stabilityHigh. Long hydrophobic chains interact and form a rigid core, so break-up is slow (hours) and drug release takes minutes to hours.Low. Liquid-like core, so break-up is fast (milliseconds).
Why this matters after IV injection: injected micelles are diluted in blood. (1) Because the CMC is so low, polymeric micelles may stay above the CMC even after dilution. (2) Even if they fall below the CMC, their rigid core makes them disassemble slowly. Surfactant micelles fall apart almost instantly and dump the drug. Polymeric micelles can therefore give sustained release and, eventually, drug targeting.

8.4 Loading drug into polymeric micelles

  1. Dialysis: dissolve polymer and drug in a water-miscible organic solvent (DMSO or DMF) and dialyze against water. Water gradually replaces the solvent, micelles form, and the drug is trapped in the core.
  2. O/W emulsion: make the micelles first (e.g., by solvent displacement), then add the drug dissolved in a water-immiscible solvent (chloroform). Mix vigorously so the drug moves into the core, then evaporate the chloroform.
  3. Solvent evaporation / film hydration (as for liposomes): dissolve drug and polymer in a solvent such as methanol, evaporate to a film, then add water to get drug-loaded micelles.
  4. Co-solvent evaporation (also called solvent precipitation or displacement): use a water-miscible, low-boiling solvent (acetone, acetonitrile or THF) to dissolve polymer and drug. Add it to water, or add water dropwise to it, then evaporate the acetone.
  5. Microfluidics: increasingly used for controlled self-assembly.

9. One-Page Cheat Sheet

  1. Cyclodextrins = hydrophobic cavity, inclusion complex, good solubilizer; fast release, so no targeting.
  2. Liposome: hydrophilic drug in the core, hydrophobic in the bilayer, amphiphilic at the interface. Cholesterol ↑ rigidity and ↓ leakage.
  3. Preparation: film hydration (then sonicate/extrude), ethanol injection (smaller, no extrusion), microfluidics (scalable).
  4. Remote loading (Doxil): ammonium sulfate inside, doxorubicin precipitates, very low leakage.
  5. Only sustained release changes PK/BD. Slow release = PK of the carrier. Slow release is a prerequisite for targeting.
  6. Conventional liposomes: opsonins, then MPS/Kupffer cells, so short circulation and liver/spleen uptake. Stealth: PEG hydration layer, long circulation, EPR.
  7. Doxil: ↓CL, ↓Vd (254 L → 4.1 L), ↑t½ (>50 h), ↑AUC, ↑tumor. ↓ cardiotoxicity; ↑ mucositis and hand-foot syndrome (delay the next cycle).
  8. Ligands don't increase tumor accumulation, can ruin stealth, and only change intratumoral distribution. Watch for the binding site barrier. Attach ligands by post-insertion.
  9. ThermoDox failed because it leaked at 37 °C.
  10. Polymer NPs: O/W for hydrophobic drugs, W/O/W for hydrophilic drugs. An immiscible solvent means the emulsion method; a miscible solvent means solvent displacement (smaller particles). Dialysis purification risks burst release.
  11. Micelles: in water, formation is entropy-driven and endothermic (hydrophobic effect). Compared with surfactant micelles: lower CMC and higher kinetic stability.
  12. Genexol-PM (PEG-PDLLA paclitaxel) succeeded with no Cremophor toxicity. NK911, SP1049C and NK105 failed.

1. What are cyclodextrins, how do they solubilize drugs, and why did they fail for targeting? Cyclic oligosaccharides of 6 (α), 7 (β) or 8 (γ) glucopyranose units with a hydrophilic exterior and a hydrophobic cavity. Poorly soluble drugs enter the cavity and form 1:1 or 1:2 inclusion complexes, increasing their aqueous solubility. They failed for targeting because drug release from the complex is very fast, so the drug does not follow the carrier.
2. Where are hydrophilic, hydrophobic and amphiphilic drugs located in a liposome? Why is cholesterol added? Hydrophilic drugs are in the aqueous core, hydrophobic drugs in the lipid bilayer, and amphiphilic drugs at the bilayer–water interface (e.g., amphotericin B). Cholesterol increases bilayer rigidity and reduces drug leakage.
3. Describe the thin-film hydration method. How is liposome size reduced? Lipids (± a lipophilic drug) are dissolved in an organic solvent such as chloroform. The solvent is removed by rotary evaporation, leaving a thin lipid film. The film is hydrated with an aqueous buffer (± a hydrophilic drug) with agitation, and liposomes, mainly large MLVs, form spontaneously. Size is reduced and made more uniform by sonication or extrusion.
4. Compare film hydration with ethanol injection and microfluidics. In ethanol injection, lipid dissolved in ethanol is injected into water. This gives smaller, more homogeneous liposomes, usually without extrusion or sonication. Microfluidics mixes the organic and aqueous phases better, is automated and scales up. Film hydration gives large, heterogeneous MLVs that need downsizing.
5. What is active (remote) loading? Give an example. The liposome is formed first and then incubated with the drug. A buffer (creating a pH gradient) or a salt inside the liposome drives the drug inward and traps it by ionization or precipitation. Example: Doxil, where ammonium sulfate inside forms a doxorubicin–sulfate complex that precipitates, greatly reducing leakage.
6. What quality attributes must a generic liposome (e.g., generic Doxil) meet? Lipid analysis (concentration), drug encapsulation, particle size, polydispersity and charge, stability (aggregation and shelf life), and drug release. These ensure batch-to-batch uniformity and similarity to the brand product.
7. What is field-flow fractionation and its advantage? AF4 separates nanoparticles by size (or density, or charge if an electric field is applied) based only on their movement in a channel flow with a cross-flow. Its advantage is that there is no stationary phase, so separation depends on flow alone and is gentle.
8. How is in vitro release measured, and why add surfactant or albumin? Release is usually measured by dialysis, which can be run in USP apparatus 2; other options are sample-and-separate, continuous flow and reverse dialysis. Either the medium is sampled, or one dialysis sample is used per time point and the drug remaining inside is measured. Surfactant or albumin solubilizes or binds poorly soluble drugs so that sink conditions are maintained.
9. Rapid- vs sustained-release liposomes: why is slow release a prerequisite for targeting? In rapid-release liposomes the hydrophobic drug sits in the bilayer and leaves fast, so its PK is like the free drug's. In sustained-release liposomes the drug is in the aqueous core, and only then can the carrier change PK and biodistribution. Very slow release gives drug PK ≈ carrier PK; very fast release gives free-drug PK; sustained release falls in between. The slower the release, the more the drug follows the carrier to the target, so slow release is required for targeting.
10. How can a sustained-release liposome increase the therapeutic index? The drug follows the liposome, which accumulates at disease sites through EPR and avoids normal tissues. More drug at the target lowers the effective dose, and less exposure of normal tissue raises the toxic dose. TI (toxic dose ÷ effective dose) therefore increases.
11. What are the problems with conventional liposomes? Give examples. Opsonins adsorb to the bare surface, and the liposomes are taken up by the MPS (Kupffer cells in the liver, and the spleen). The result is short circulation, failure to localize in diseased tissue, and high liver/spleen uptake with possible toxicity on repeated dosing. Examples: Myocet (doxorubicin) and AmBisome (amphotericin B, which reduces nephrotoxicity).
12. How do stealth liposomes avoid the MPS? Surface PEG binds water and forms a hydration layer that prevents opsonin adsorption. The liposome isn't recognized as foreign by macrophages, so liver and spleen uptake drops and circulation is long. Combined with sustained release, this leads to accumulation in tumors or inflamed tissue via EPR.
13. Describe Doxil and the PK changes of doxorubicin in stealth liposomes. Doxil is a PEGylated SUV liposome of doxorubicin: about 100 nm, made of HSPC and cholesterol, remote-loaded, with t½ over 50 h. Its main benefit is reduced cardiotoxicity. Compared with free doxorubicin it gives ↓ clearance, ↓ Vd, ↑ half-life, ↑ AUC and ↑ distribution to tumors.
14. Why does Vd fall from 254 L to 4.1 L? Vd = amount of drug in the body ÷ plasma concentration. Free doxorubicin distributes widely into tissues, giving a large Vd. Liposomes are too large to cross the continuous capillaries of normal tissues and, being stealth, aren't taken up by macrophages, so the drug is confined to plasma. Vd then approaches blood volume (about 5 L).
15. Which doxorubicin side effects decrease and which increase with Doxil? Explain hand-foot syndrome. Decreased: cardiotoxicity, hematological toxicity, hair loss, neuropathy, vesicant injury, nausea and vomiting. Increased: mucositis and hand-foot syndrome (PPE). Hand-foot syndrome is thought to occur because liposomes are squeezed out of capillaries at pressure points in the hands and feet. It appears at doses above about 10–12 mg/m²/week and is managed by delaying the next chemotherapy cycle.
16. Why is Doxil the most successful formulation at changing drug PK? Doxorubicin is complexed and precipitated with sulfate inside the stealth liposome, so leakage is minimal. The drug therefore follows the long-circulating carrier.
17. What is Onivyde, and why isn't SN-38 given directly? Onivyde is PEGylated liposomal irinotecan (2015). Irinotecan is a water-soluble prodrug of SN-38. SN-38 is about 1000× more potent but too poorly soluble to inject. The liposome changes the PK of irinotecan and its metabolites much as Doxil does (↑ AUC, ↑ t½).
18. Does attaching a ligand increase tumor accumulation of liposomes? No. Tumor localization is governed by circulation time and EPR. A high ligand density can mask the PEG layer and increase clearance, lowering tumor accumulation. Ligands may change intratumoral distribution (e.g., cell internalization), and internalizing ligands generally work better than non-internalizing ones.
19. What is the binding site barrier? In large tumors, ligand-targeted liposomes bind the first layer of tumor cells near the vessels and are trapped there, which prevents penetration into the tumor core. The larger the tumor, the greater the barrier. Micrometastases and intravascular targets present fewer barriers.
20. Describe post-insertion and why it is preferred. The ligand is coupled to a PEG-phospholipid such as maleimide-PEG-DSPE, and these molecules form micelles. The micelles are co-incubated with preformed liposomes (e.g., 37 °C for 1 h), and the ligand-lipids insert into the outer leaflet. This exposes more ligand on the surface than mixing it in before film hydration or ethanol injection, where many ligands end up facing the interior.
21. What is ThermoDox and why did it fail? ThermoDox is a lysolipid-containing, thermosensitive liposomal doxorubicin designed to release drug rapidly at 42 °C under local hyperthermia. It failed because it did not prevent doxorubicin leakage at 37 °C.
22. Nanocapsule vs nanosphere? A nanocapsule is a reservoir: a drug core surrounded by a polymer shell. A nanosphere is a matrix: drug dispersed throughout the polymer.
23. Describe emulsification/solvent evaporation. When do you use O/W vs W/O/W? A polymer such as PLGA is dissolved in a water-immiscible solvent (chloroform) and emulsified into an aqueous phase containing PVA or surfactant. Evaporating the solvent hardens the droplets into nanoparticles stabilized by PVA. Use O/W for water-insoluble drugs and W/O/W (double emulsion) for water-soluble drugs.
24. How does solvent displacement differ from the emulsion method and from salting out? Solvent displacement uses a water-miscible solvent (acetone, THF, acetonitrile); water acts as a non-solvent and precipitates the polymer, giving smaller particles than emulsion methods. Salting out is similar, but precipitation is triggered by electrolytes in the aqueous phase.
25. Describe coacervation. Used mainly for microparticles and reservoir systems. The polymer is dissolved in an organic solvent containing dispersed drug particles. A non-solvent (water) is added, and the polymer separates as a coacervate that coats the drug core. The solvent is then evaporated and the coat hardens.
26. How are nanoparticles purified, and what is the drawback of dialysis? Methods: dialysis, ultrafiltration (Amicon filters with vacuum or centrifugation), ultracentrifugation (particles pellet, free drug stays in the supernatant), and SEC/GPC (Sephadex; large particles elute first). Drawback of dialysis: the drug can migrate to the particle surface, causing burst release later.
27. What is Abraxane, and how does it differ from Doxil? Abraxane is albumin-bound paclitaxel nanoparticles (2005, breast cancer). Unlike Doxil, it does not change paclitaxel's PK the way a stealth carrier would, yet it is clinically effective and was a breakthrough.
28. What is steric stabilization and how is it achieved? Hydrophilic macromolecules are placed on the particle surface to prevent aggregation. This is done either by physical adsorption (PVA, Pluronic PEO-PPO-PEO, PEO-PLA) or by chemical grafting of PEG.
29. Describe the structure and uses of polymeric micelles. Amphiphilic block copolymers above the CMC self-assemble into about 100 nm core–shell structures with a dense hydrophilic PEG shell and a hydrophobic core. They solubilize poorly water-soluble drugs and can be used for targeted delivery.
30. Explain why micellization in water is entropy-driven. ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, and ΔG must be negative. In water, micellization is endothermic (ΔH > 0), so a large entropy increase is needed. Hydrophobic blocks can't hydrogen-bond with water; they disrupt its 3D hydrogen-bond network and order the surrounding water. Clustering the hydrophobic blocks into a core releases that ordered water and restores the network, increasing water entropy so that TΔS > ΔH and ΔG < 0. In organic solvents, micellization is enthalpy-driven.
31. Why are polymeric micelles more stable in blood than surfactant micelles? (1) A much lower CMC, because the macromolecular hydrophobic block associates strongly, so the micelles stay above the CMC after dilution. (2) Higher kinetic stability: the rigid core of interacting chains disassembles slowly (hours) even below the CMC, versus milliseconds for liquid-like surfactant cores (Cremophor EL, SDS, polysorbates). This allows sustained release and targeting after IV injection.
32. Describe two methods for loading drug into polymeric micelles. Dialysis: polymer and drug are dissolved in DMSO or DMF and dialyzed against water; water replaces the solvent, micelles form and the drug is loaded. Co-solvent evaporation: polymer and drug are dissolved in acetone (or acetonitrile/THF), mixed with water, and the acetone is evaporated. Other methods: O/W emulsion, film hydration and microfluidics.
33. Name clinical polymeric micelles and their outcomes. NK911 (PEG-poly(aspartic acid)/doxorubicin): PK inferior to Doxil, dropped. SP1049C (Pluronic/doxorubicin, for resistant cancers): failed. NK105 (paclitaxel): improved PK but missed clinical endpoints. Genexol-PM (PEG-PDLLA paclitaxel): marketed in South Korea and India.
34. What is the advantage of Genexol-PM over Taxol? It solubilizes paclitaxel about 5000× above its water solubility without Cremophor EL, avoiding the Cremophor-related toxicity of Taxol.
